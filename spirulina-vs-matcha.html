Spirulina vs. matcha.

Both are green and good for you — but they're after completely different things. Matcha is a caffeinated green tea for calm energy; spirulina is a caffeine-free whole food for protein and iron. Here's how they really compare.

The short version

A drink vs. a food.

Matcha is stone-ground green tea leaf, whisked into water or milk. People drink it for a calm caffeine lift — caffeine paired with the amino acid L-theanine — and for EGCG antioxidants. Spirulina is a blue-green algae eaten as a food: complete protein, iron and the antioxidant phycocyanin, with no caffeine at all. One wakes you up; the other feeds you.

Which to pick

So which is right for you?

Choose spirulina if you want…

  • Complete protein and dense plant iron
  • Nutrition with no caffeine, any time of day
  • The blue antioxidant phycocyanin
  • A flavor-free add-in for smoothies

Choose matcha if you want…

  • A calm caffeine lift for focus
  • The L-theanine “focused energy” feel
  • EGCG green-tea antioxidants
  • A warm or iced drink ritual

Because they barely overlap, the easiest answer is often both: matcha in the morning for the lift, a fresh-frozen spirulina cube in a smoothie for the protein and iron. Common questions

Spirulina vs. matcha, answered.

Is spirulina or matcha better?

They're for different reasons. Matcha gives a calm caffeine lift and EGCG antioxidants; spirulina gives caffeine-free complete protein, iron and phycocyanin. Matcha for focused energy in a drink, spirulina for nutrition without caffeine.

Does spirulina have caffeine?

No. Spirulina is naturally caffeine-free, so you can use it any time of day, including the evening — and it's a good choice if you're cutting back on caffeine.

Can I take spirulina and matcha together?

Yes — they pair well since they don't overlap. For example a matcha latte for the caffeine, plus a fresh-frozen spirulina cube in a smoothie for protein and iron. The only caffeine comes from the matcha.

Which has more antioxidants?

Both are antioxidant-rich but with different compounds — matcha's catechins (EGCG) versus spirulina's phycocyanin, chlorophyll and beta-carotene — so there's no clean winner. They complement each other.

Is “blue matcha” the same as spirulina?

No. “Blue matcha” is usually butterfly pea flower (not tea at all), and the bright-blue “blue majik” powder is an isolated spirulina extract — just the phycocyanin. SimpliiGood is whole fresh-frozen spirulina, not a single stripped-out pigment.
